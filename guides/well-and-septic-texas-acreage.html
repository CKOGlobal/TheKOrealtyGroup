Well and Septic on Texas Acreage — What to Inspect, What to Ask For, Who Regulates It

Kelli Owens · Texas REALTOR® #616346

"There's no water bill."

There isn't. There is also no water department. On a private well and septic system you are the utility, the treatment plant and the sewer district, and the records that a city would keep are records you need to ask for before you close.

Municipal versus private, in one paragraph

On city water and sewer, someone else tests the water, maintains the mains and treats the waste, and you pay a monthly bill for it. On a well and septic system you own all of that equipment: the well, the pump, the pressure tank, any softener or filtration, the septic tank, and the field or spray area that disposes of what leaves the house. None of it is bad. All of it has a service history, and you want that history.

The well

Who regulates it. Water well drillers and pump installers are licensed by the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation. When a well is drilled, the driller files a State of Texas Well Report showing depth, casing and date, and those reports are searchable through the Texas Water Development Board.

In Harris and Galveston counties, groundwater is also regulated by the Harris-Galveston Subsidence District. Wells in the district must be registered before they are drilled. A well with a casing under five inches that serves only a single-family dwelling is exempt from the district's permit requirement, but not from registration.

Testing. Texas does not require a water test when a house sells, so it happens only if you order it. Ask an accredited laboratory for a bacteria test (total coliform and E. coli) and a basic chemistry panel. If the house has a softener or reverse osmosis system, test the raw well water, not just the tap after treatment.

Equipment. Ask the age of the pump and the pressure tank, when each was last serviced, and whether any treatment equipment is owned or leased.

The septic system

Texas calls it an on-site sewage facility, or OSSF. The rules are the TCEQ's (30 TAC Chapter 285), and permitting is handled locally. In Galveston County, that is the Galveston County Health District, which holds the permit and planning materials for permitted systems and takes records requests through its open records page. A permitted system also has an affidavit recorded in the county deed records.

Know which kind it is. A conventional system is a tank and a drain field. An aerobic system adds mechanical treatment, an alarm, disinfection and usually spray heads, and it carries ongoing maintenance requirements under the TCEQ rules. That usually means a service contract, so ask for it, and for the last service reports.

The inspection. Texas does not require a septic inspection at sale either. A proper one checks the tank and its levels, the pumps and alarms, the aerobic components if there are any, and the drain field or spray area, and it often means having the tank pumped so the inspector can see its condition.

What to ask for at the table

  • The State of Texas Well Report, or the driller's name and date if no report can be found
  • Subsidence district registration for the well
  • A recent water test, or your own, ordered during the option period
  • Age and service history of the pump, pressure tank and any treatment equipment
  • The OSSF permit and approved design from the health district
  • For an aerobic system, the maintenance contract and recent service reports
  • The seller's disclosure sections on water and sewer, and TREC's Information About On-Site Sewer Facility form

If a tract doesn't have a well

The cost of drilling is driven mostly by depth: how far down good water is at that location, the casing size, the pump, and the treatment the water turns out to need. Two bids from TDLR-licensed drillers, plus the depths of nearby wells from the state's well reports, will tell you more than any per-foot figure on the internet. Register with the subsidence district before anyone drills.

What I'd tell you

None of this is a reason to avoid well and septic. It is a reason to ask for paper. A system with a permit, a service history and a clean water test is a better answer than a city connection you know nothing about.
