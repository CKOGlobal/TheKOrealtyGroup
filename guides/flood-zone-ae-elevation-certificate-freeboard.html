Zone AE, Freeboard, and the Elevation Certificate — The Document That Changes the Conversation

Kelli Owens · Texas REALTOR® #616346

"It's in AE. Walk away."

That is the most common thing anyone on the Gulf Coast is told about a flood zone, and it skips the only question that matters for the house in front of you: how high is the floor?

What Zone AE actually means

Zone AE is FEMA's 1% annual-chance floodplain, the area people call the 100-year floodplain. It is inside the Special Flood Hazard Area, and it comes with a base flood elevation, or BFE: the height FEMA expects water to reach in that 1% flood, stated in feet above a survey datum. On current maps in this county the datum is NAVD 88.

Two consequences follow from AE, and neither one is optional:

  • Lenders must require flood insurance. A federally regulated or insured lender has to require it on a building in an A or V zone. That is the mandatory purchase requirement, and AE is under it.
  • The zone does not change because the house is high. A house built well above BFE is still in AE until FEMA revises the map or approves a change for that property.

If someone tells you a high house "isn't really in a flood zone," they are wrong, and you should hear it from me first.

What freeboard is

Freeboard is the distance between the base flood elevation and the building's lowest floor. A house with a lowest floor two feet above BFE has two feet of freeboard. Many communities require some freeboard on new and substantially improved construction; check the local floodplain ordinance, because the requirement varies.

Freeboard doesn't change the zone. It changes how far the water has to rise before it is inside the house.

Why the floor matters more than the letter

Under FEMA's current National Flood Insurance Program rating method, Risk Rating 2.0, the zone is not a premium rating factor. The building is. One of the variables that matters is the first floor height: how high the first floor sits above the ground around it.

FEMA estimates first floor height from the policy application and its own data. You can instead submit an elevation certificate, and FEMA uses whichever figure is more favorable to the policyholder. Under the NFIP, a good certificate can help and a missing one costs you the chance.

Private flood insurers set their own rules, and many ask for a certificate. Either way, the certificate gets a measured building into the quote instead of an estimated one.

What's on an elevation certificate

The certificate is FEMA's form, completed and sealed by a licensed surveyor or engineer. It records:

  • The flood zone, the FIRM panel number and its effective date
  • The base flood elevation and the datum
  • The elevation of the lowest floor, and of the ground next to the building
  • A building diagram number that describes how the house is built
  • Whether it was prepared from construction drawings, during construction, or as finished construction

That last line matters. A finished-construction certificate describes the house as it actually stands, which is the one an insurer and an underwriter will want.

What to do with one

Get it early. Ask for the certificate before your option period starts, not after your lender orders a flood determination.

Check the panel and the date. FEMA revises maps. The certificate should match the current panel.

Compare the lowest floor to the BFE. That difference is the freeboard, and it is the number the rest of this conversation turns on.

Get your own quote. Hand the certificate to your insurance agent during the option period. I don't quote premiums and neither should anyone else who isn't writing the policy.

Read the disclosure. The seller's disclosure asks about past flooding, water penetration and flood claims. The map gives you the statistics. The disclosure tells you what happened to this house.

What I'd tell you

An AE address without an elevation certificate is a question. An AE address with a finished-construction certificate showing the floor well above BFE is an answer, and it is the document that moves a buyer from "walk away" to "let me get a quote."

My current acreage listing, 103 Avenue L in Dickinson, is in Zone AE. Base flood elevation is 14.2 feet NAVD 88, and the finished floor is 16.20 feet, two feet above it, per a finished-construction elevation certificate sealed in October 2024. Flood insurance is required for a financed purchase there, and the listing says so in the body of the page, not in the fine print.

The sellers' disclosure also reports previous flooding during Hurricane Harvey in 2017, including water penetration into a structure on the property, and the listing says that plainly as well. That is exactly why the certificate and the disclosure belong side by side: one tells you how high the floor is today, the other tells you what has actually happened there.
