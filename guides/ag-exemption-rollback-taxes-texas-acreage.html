Ag Exemption and Rollback Taxes — What to Check Before You Close on Texas Acreage

Kelli Owens · Texas REALTOR® #616346

"It has an ag exemption, so the taxes are low."

That sentence is true right up until you close, and then it is a question about you. Before you buy acreage in Texas, find out whether the land carries a special valuation, what it would take to keep it, and what happens if you don't.

It isn't an exemption

What everyone calls an "ag exemption" is a special appraisal. Most often it is 1-d-1 open-space valuation, under Chapter 23 of the Texas Tax Code. Instead of taxing the land on what it would sell for, the appraisal district taxes it on what it can produce: grazing, hay, crops, timber, or qualifying wildlife management.

On acreage near a city, the gap between those two numbers can be enormous. That gap is the whole benefit, and it is also the whole risk.

To qualify, the land has to be used principally for agriculture to the degree of intensity that is normal in the area, and it has to have been used that way for five of the preceding seven years. A few horses on a big lot, or a hay field nobody has cut since the previous owner, may not meet that bar.

It doesn't come with the deed

The valuation belongs to the use, not to the land. When ownership changes, the appraisal district can require the new owner to file a new application, and in practice it usually does. If you aren't going to keep running cattle or cutting hay, you aren't going to keep the valuation.

What "rollback" means now

When land under 1-d-1 valuation changes to a non-qualifying use, the county imposes an additional tax: the difference between what was paid on the productivity value and what would have been paid on market value, for each of the three years before the year of the change. A tax lien attaches to the land the day the use changes.

If you have read that rollback is "five years plus seven percent interest," that was the law before September 1, 2019. That year the Legislature cut it to three years at five percent (HB 1743). In 2021 it removed the interest entirely (HB 3833, effective June 15, 2021). Interest and penalties now apply only if the additional tax becomes delinquent.

Three years of the difference is still a real number on acreage near Houston. It is just smaller than the version still circulating online.

What counts as a change of use

Stopping the qualifying use. Building on land that was in production, subdividing it, fencing off pasture for a yard, or simply letting the hay lease lapse and not replacing it with another qualifying use. The appraisal district decides, and it decides after you own the land.

Who pays it

Read your contract. The standard TREC residential contracts put it on the buyer: if the sale, or the buyer's use of the property after closing, results in additional taxes for periods before closing, those are the buyer's obligation. You can negotiate that differently, but only before you sign.

That is why this belongs in your option period, not in your first tax bill.

How to check, in ten minutes

Pull the appraisal district record. Look for an agricultural, open-space or 1-d-1 notation, and for two values: a productivity value and a market value. If only a market value appears, there is no special valuation to lose.

Ask the seller, in writing, whether any part of the property is under agricultural, timber or wildlife valuation, and whether there is a current grazing or hay lease.

Ask the title company to show any rollback or additional-tax exposure on the tax certificate.

If there is a valuation and you want to keep it, ask the appraisal district what it will require from you, before closing, not after.

What I'd tell you

A low tax bill on acreage is either a valuation you are going to keep working for, or a number that is going to reset the year you stop. Neither is bad. Not knowing which one you are buying is.

My current acreage listing, 103 Avenue L in Dickinson, carries no agricultural valuation on the 2026 appraisal roll, so there is no rollback exposure on a change of use. That is the answer you want in writing on any tract, and the appraisal district record is where you get it.
