A house I listed recently — [110 Cloudbridge Drive in League City](https://110cloudbridge.com/) — has three
square footage numbers attached to it.

The appraisal district says **2,385**. The measured floor plan from the 3D scan says **2,205**.
That's a 180 square foot gap — about 7.5% — on the same house, measured by people who both knew
what they were doing.

Neither one is wrong. They're answering different questions. And if you don't know that going
in, you will either overpay for space that isn't there or talk yourself out of a house over a
discrepancy that means nothing.

## The three numbers

**The appraisal district number** is gross area measured from the *outside* of the exterior
walls, usually from a plat, a permit, or a drive-by. Nobody from the CAD has been inside your
house. This is the number the MLS almost always publishes, because it's the only one that
exists for every property in the county and it's free.

**The floor plan number** — the kind a 3D scan or a floor-plan service produces — is typically
gross *internal* area. Measured wall face to wall face, inside. On a house with 2x4 framing,
brick veneer, and a lot of exterior wall, that's a meaningful amount of thickness you just
stopped counting.

**The appraiser's number** is the one that matters for your loan. Appraisers measure to a
professional standard that counts finished, heated-and-cooled living area, applies consistent
rules about what qualifies, and gets measured on site. It usually lands closer to the floor
plan figure than to the CAD figure.

On my League City listing: 1,513 down and 872 up per the appraisal district, versus 1,421 and
784 measured internally. The gap is almost entirely wall thickness, and it's larger on the
first floor because there's more exterior wall down there.

## Why this matters more than it sounds like it should

**Price per square foot comps get noisy.** If you're comparing $/sq ft across listings, you're
comparing numbers from inconsistent sources. Most come from the CAD, some come from a builder's
plan, occasionally one comes from an actual measurement. That's not an apples-to-apples column
even when it looks like one.

**Appraisals come in on a different number than the listing.** This is routine and usually
harmless, because the appraiser applies the same standard to the comps. Problems start when a
listing published an inflated figure from a source nobody disclosed.

**Buyers feel misled.** This is the real cost. Somebody reads 2,385, walks through, likes it,
then sees 2,205 on the appraisal and wonders what else was oversold. Nothing was — but that
conversation is now happening in week five instead of week one.

## What a listing should do

Publish the number *and* the source. "2,385 sq ft per the Galveston Central Appraisal District"
takes four extra words and eliminates the entire problem. Anyone who cares can go verify, and
anyone who doesn't isn't surprised later.

If a house has a measured floor plan, publish that too. Two numbers with two sources reads as
transparent. One number with no source reads as marketing.

## What to do as a buyer

**Ask where the number came from.** If the answer is "the MLS," that means the appraisal
district, which means gross exterior.

**If square footage drives your decision, measure it.** An appraisal will do it. So will a
floor plan service, for a couple hundred dollars, before you spend money on an inspection.

**Don't argue with the appraiser using the CAD number.** They measure to a standard; the
appraisal district estimates from the curb. That argument does not go where you want it to go.

**Check the split, not just the total.** On two-story houses the down/up allocation matters more
than the total for how the house actually lives. A 2,385 with 1,513 down feels completely
different from a 2,385 with 1,100 down.

## What I'd actually tell you

Every listing in Texas publishes a number that came from a government office that never went
inside. That's not a conspiracy, it's just the only universally available data.

Know which number you're looking at, ask for the source, and if the difference between 2,205
and 2,385 changes your answer on a house — measure it. That's a $200 question, not a $350,000
one.
