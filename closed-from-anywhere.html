Why it matters that I'm also a notary

I've been on the other side of the signing.

Arranging a remote or mobile closing isn't rare — your title company does it all the time, and I'll never pretend otherwise. What is uncommon is having an agent who has actually performed notarizations and knows what the hour looks like from the notary's chair.

That's the whole difference. I'm not selling you a service the title company already provides. I'm telling you what's coming before you sit down.

What I prepare you for

  • Knowledge-based authentication. A short quiz built from your credit history, and it is the single most common reason a remote signing stalls. Thin files, recent moves and frozen credit all trip it. I tell you what it asks and what to do if it fails, before you're sitting there watching it fail.
  • What has to be wet ink. Not everything can be signed electronically. Which documents, and whether your lender and county allow what your title company allows, is a question with a real answer — I get it in writing early instead of three days before closing.
  • Your ID, and whose name is on it. Expired license, a name that doesn't match the deed, a maiden name on the trust. All fixable in week one. None of it fixable at 4pm on closing day.
  • How long it actually takes, and who has to be present. Both spouses, a power of attorney that has to be pre-approved by the underwriter, a co-signer in another state. These get sorted at contract, not at signing.

And when you need a notary for something outside a transaction I'm part of — a power of attorney, an affidavit, estate paperwork — I can be that notary, or point you to one.

The process

What a remote signing actually looks like.

Twenty to forty minutes, from wherever you have a camera and a decent connection. Whoever performs it — your title company's notary, or me when it's a document outside a transaction I'm in — this is the shape of it.

  1. We confirm your title company accepts RON. This happens first, not last. Acceptance depends on the title company, the lender, and whether the county e-records. I confirm it in writing before we schedule anything, so nobody discovers a problem on signing day.
  2. You upload the documents and verify your identity. Government-issued ID plus knowledge-based authentication through the state-approved platform. This is the step that trips people up; I walk you through it beforehand.
  3. We meet on live video and you sign. I am physically in Texas, as the law requires. You are wherever you are. The session is recorded and retained as the statute requires.
  4. The executed package goes straight to title. Electronically sealed and delivered. No overnight envelope, no wet-ink original crossing the country, no waiting on a courier.

Straight answers

Before you ask.

"Is this actually legal, or is it a workaround?"
It is statutory. Texas passed remote online notarization into law in 2017 and expanded it in 2019, and the Secretary of State maintains the list of approved technology providers. It is not a pandemic-era exception that expired.
"Can every closing be done this way?"
No — and I will tell you that early. Acceptance depends on your title company, your lender, and whether the county e-records. All-cash purchases and seller-side packages are the most widely accepted. Some lenders still require wet ink. I confirm before we schedule, not after.
"Do I need special software?"
A browser, a camera and your ID. The platform runs in the browser. The piece worth preparing for is knowledge-based authentication — a short quiz built from your credit history. I'll tell you what to expect so it doesn't catch you cold.
"You're my agent and a notary — can you just notarize my closing?"
No, and you should be suspicious of anyone who says yes. A commission is a disqualifying financial interest under Texas law. A notary is supposed to be a neutral witness, and I am not neutral about a transaction I am paid on. On my own listings an independent notary handles the signing — and I make sure the remote closing is actually possible before we schedule it, which is the part that usually goes wrong.
"Can you notarize things that aren't real estate?"
Yes. Powers of attorney, affidavits, parental travel consents, corporate resolutions, and similar documents — subject to the same Texas rules.
